1. From Genealogy to Biography Networks
Traditional genealogical systems such as those based on GEDCOM organize data primarily around families and a fixed set of events. These models were created for lineage reconstruction, not for describing the full context of human lives. Their limitations become evident when one tries to represent movement across space and time, overlapping relationships, or personal histories outside the conventional monogamous family frame.
Bonsai redefines this foundation.
Instead of “individuals in families,” Bonsai treats people as agents in a living web of episodes — interconnected across geography, chronology, and social relations. This change turns a genealogical database into a biographical knowledge network, where the narrative, not the schema, is central.

2. The Concept of Episodes
An Episode in Bonsai is a flexible unit describing something that happened, involving one or more people, over a span of time, at one or more places.
Examples include:
    • Birth, death, marriage, adoption (classical events)
    • Migration, education, service, illness, journey, discovery (biographical expansions)
    • Cohabitation, divorce, partnership, friendship (social and emotional links)
    • Occupation, property ownership, membership, or creative works (economic and cultural aspects)
Each episode integrates:
    • When it occurred (via Gnomon: precise, fuzzy, or interval dates)
    • Where it occurred (via Orbis: structured, hierarchical places)
    • Who was involved (persons and their roles)
    • How it is known (linked sources, notes, provenance)
Episodes thus form the temporal and spatial backbone of a person’s biography.

4. Openness and Extensibility
Bonsai includes a core ontology of common episode types (birth, death, marriage, migration, occupation) but allows users to extend the vocabulary freely:
    • Users may define new episode types with custom names, icons, and semantics.
    • Ontologies may be imported/exported as JSON, shared among projects, or aligned with external vocabularies (e.g., CIDOC-CRM, Dublin Core).
This open structure supports everything from private family research to scholarly biographical datasets and local history archives.

5. A System of Contexts
By merging episodes, places, and times, Bonsai allows a holistic reconstruction of a person’s life in its context. Users can:
    • Follow a person’s movements across geography and chronology.
    • View networks of co-participants (families, colleagues, witnesses, neighbors).
    • Filter by time, place, or thematic tags.
    • Visualize how individual lives overlap and diverge within a community.
The system thus supports narrative exploration, not only data management — a step beyond conventional genealogy.

6. Compatibility and Pragmatism
While GEDCOM import/export will remain integral, Bonsai’s internal model deliberately transcends it.
    • Classical GEDCOM data are mapped into Bonsai’s Episode model (e.g., BIRT, DEAT, MARR → predefined episode types).
    • Extended or custom episodes are retained internally but exportable via annotations or custom tags for backward compatibility.
    • This ensures Bonsai can coexist with traditional genealogy tools while maintaining its richer expressivity.

7. Vision Summary
Bonsai reimagines genealogy as biography.
It focuses not only on who begat whom, but on who lived where, when, and how — an open, extensible framework where life stories unfold in time and space.
